# Should I focus on the interest rate or the monthly payment when buying a house?

Focus on the payment, because it's the number you live with every month, and a lower rate bought with points is only worth it if you keep the loan long enough to earn back what you paid for it. The Lindley Team, David and Bri, will show you the mortgage payment both ways, with and without the extra cash up front, and tell you which we would pick.

*By The Lindley Team. Last updated October 19, 2026.*

If you've got two loan offers open side by side and you aren't sure what you're actually supposed to prioritize, start with the mortgage payment. The rate is one ingredient in it. The payment is what decides whether you can take a vacation, absorb a roof repair, or get through a slow month without lying awake over it.

## What "get the lowest rate" covers

"Get the lowest rate" is the comfortable advice in our business. It's easy to say and easy to advertise. What it leaves out is that a lower rate can be bought. You pay points at closing, cash up front, to bring the rate down. One point equals one percent of the loan amount, according to the federal Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB). So the lowest rate on a page can be the one you paid the most for on closing day.

## What if the payment I qualify for isn't one I can live on?

It's the question under a lot of the others. A lender can qualify you for more than you'd want to pay every month, because qualifying and affording are not the same thing, and only you can decide what you can afford. That's how people end up house poor: the loan works on paper and the day to day doesn't.

So decide what payment fits your actual life first. The rest of this page is about how to get there.

## Points or cash: the break-even

When you're weighing points, the break-even is the thing to ask for. It's arithmetic:

**What the points cost up front ÷ how much they lower your payment each month = the number of months before they've paid for themselves.**

Put another way, the break-even is the point where the monthly savings finally exceed the extra closing costs of the lower rate. If you sell or refinance before then, you paid for savings you didn't keep. We run that number for you; you don't have to.

The CFPB suggests asking for loan options with and without points and comparing the total cost over a few timeframes: a short one, a long one, and the length of time you realistically expect to keep the loan. That's the comparison we'll put in front of you.

| | Lower rate by paying points | Keep the cash |
|---|---|---|
| Mortgage payment | Lower | Unchanged |
| Cash needed at closing | More | Less |
| What it protects | Keeping the loan a long time, past the break-even | Your savings after closing, for the first year's surprises |
| What it risks | You move or refinance before the break-even | A higher payment for as long as you keep the loan |
| Mortgage insurance (PMI) | Cash spent on points isn't going toward your down payment, and on a conventional loan with less than 20% down, PMI applies | Cash kept can sit in reserves, or go toward a down payment; at 20% down, a conventional loan doesn't need PMI |

If you do put less than 20 percent down on a conventional loan, PMI isn't forever. You can ask to cancel it once your loan balance reaches 80 percent of the home's original value with a good payment history, and it ends automatically at 78 percent.

## Which one we'd pick

In our view, if your repair fund is empty, keep the cash. The same goes for a first-time buyer with thin reserves, and for anyone who might move in a few years. If you plan to stay a long time and you'll still have reserves after closing, buying the rate down might make sense for you.

## What you control

The price of the house sets much of your payment, and that's where your choices are. You control the trade: the number of bedrooms, the number of bathrooms, a house or a townhome or a condo, the size of the lot or yard, and the location. ## Frequently asked questions

**Should I focus on the interest rate or the monthly payment when buying a house?**
Focus on the payment, because it's the number you live with every month. The rate is one part of it, and a lower rate bought with points only pays off if you keep the loan past the break-even.

**Is it worth buying the rate down with points or keeping the cash?**
It's worth it only if you'll keep the loan long enough to earn back what the points cost. Divide the cost of the points by how much they lower your payment each month to get the months to break even; if you might move or refinance sooner, or your reserves are thin, keeping the cash protects you better.

**How do I avoid being house poor when I buy my first home?**
Decide what payment fits your day to day before you settle on a price, and keep cash for the first year's surprises. Qualifying for a loan and affording the payment are different questions, and the trade-offs you control, such as bedrooms, house or condo, yard and location, are how you make the payment fit.

**How much does my monthly payment change if I put more money down?**
More down means a smaller loan and a lower payment, and on a conventional loan, reaching 20 percent down means you don't need PMI. We'll show you the payment at each down payment you're weighing, next to what it leaves you in reserves.

**What should I give up to make a Portland house fit my budget: bedrooms, yard, or location?**
That choice is yours, and it's the part of the decision you control. The usual trades are bedrooms, bathrooms, a house versus a townhome or condo, the size of the lot or yard, and location, and we'll run the payment for each so you can compare.
