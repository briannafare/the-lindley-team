# Can I get or keep a mortgage during a divorce?

Possibly, if the spouse keeping the house can qualify for the loan on their own income, though credit and the settlement terms matter too. The Lindley Team's Bri Lindley, a Certified Divorce Lending Professional (CDLP), starts with a conversation before the settlement is signed.

*By The Lindley Team. Last updated October 19, 2026.*

We can't tell you from this page whether you'll keep the house. Nobody can until the numbers have been run on one income, against the settlement you're being asked to sign. What we can do is run them early, while the settlement is still a draft and can still change.

Your attorney decides the settlement terms. We check whether the mortgage can carry them.

And one worry you can set down now. A lender looks at you as an individual applicant, and federal rules protect your right to credit in your own name whatever your marital status.

## What is a CDLP, and when should you bring one in?

CDLP stands for Certified Divorce Lending Professional. Put plainly, it's a loan officer trained in where divorce and mortgages meet: the basics of family law, how property gets divided, and how a lender will treat the income in a settlement. The Divorce Lending Association issues the designation. The association was founded in 2014 and reports more than 1,000 certified professionals. Bri holds the designation.

A CDLP works on the lending side while the settlement is still a draft. The job is to test whether a buyout, or one spouse keeping the house, can be financed, to work out which settlement income will count toward qualifying, and to flag any settlement language a lender can't carry out. That's why the time to bring one in is before the settlement is signed, because after that the terms are what they are.

## Can you keep the house? How a buyout refinance works

The divorce decree can give you the house. Federal law says a lender can't call the loan due just because the house passed to a spouse through a divorce decree or separation agreement.

A new loan in the keeping spouse's name is the buyout refinance. The spouse keeping the house takes a mortgage in their own name only, the old loan is paid off, and the other spouse is paid their share of the equity.

Fannie Mae sets the rules many conventional loans follow. Here's what it requires for a buyout that comes out of a divorce settlement:

| What the rule says | Who it applies to | Source |
|---|---|---|
| It counts as a limited cash-out refinance if the house was jointly owned for at least 12 months before the new loan funds | The property | Fannie Mae Selling Guide B2-1.3-02 |
| Everyone involved signs a written agreement stating the terms of the transfer and where the refinance money goes | Both spouses | Fannie Mae Selling Guide B2-1.3-02 |
| The spouse keeping the house qualifies for the new loan on their own | The keeping spouse | Fannie Mae Selling Guide B2-1.3-02 |
| The spouse keeping the house can't receive any of the refinance proceeds | The keeping spouse | Fannie Mae Selling Guide B2-1.3-02 |
| An appraisal sets the value the buyout is figured from | The property | The Lindley Team |

Here's how the equity math works. This is an illustration, not a quote. A home worth $600,000 with a $300,000 mortgage has $300,000 in equity, so each spouse's share is $150,000. The spouse keeping the house would refinance for $450,000: the $300,000 mortgage plus the $150,000 buyout. Your real numbers start with the appraisal.

### When the answer is no

A buyout refinance is a new loan, and new loans have closing costs. Qualifying on one income is harder than qualifying on two. If the spouse keeping the house can't qualify alone, the answer is no, and we'll tell you so before the settlement is signed, while there's still time to change it. A house can also be possible on paper and too tight to live with on one income, and we'll tell you that too. The timeline depends on the divorce itself, which nobody on the lending side controls.

## Can you qualify using alimony or child support?

Yes, if it meets the rules. Fannie Mae's guideline asks for four things. The payments have to be expected to continue for at least three years from the date you apply. You need at least six months of full, regular, on-time payments already received. A court decree or separation agreement has to show the amount and the terms. And the payments have to show up as received, in bank statements, cancelled checks or electronic records.

A one-time payment that evens out the property split is different. Fannie Mae calls it a lump-sum equalization payment, and it doesn't count as steady income.

So if your support has only just started, the six months may not be there yet, and that affects when you can apply. It's one of the things we look at in the first conversation.

## Should you refinance before or after the divorce is final?

The analysis comes first, and it can start before the divorce is final. The refinance itself happens after, once the decree spells out the terms. The order matters because whatever the settlement says about the house has to be something a lender can fund. An agreement can look good on paper and still be impossible to finance, and that's much easier to fix while it's a draft.

If you're the spouse buying a new home, the same timing holds. You can start as soon as the divorce is final, and if the terms are clear enough, the pre-approval can sometimes begin during the divorce.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a CDLP, and do I need one?**
A CDLP is a Certified Divorce Lending Professional: a loan officer trained in how divorce settlements, support income and property division affect a mortgage. It matters most when the house or a new mortgage is part of the settlement, and the time to bring one in is before the settlement is signed.

**Can I keep the house in a divorce?**
Possibly. You would need to qualify for a new loan on your own income, pay your spouse their share of the equity, and carry the costs of the house alone. We run those numbers early and tell you whether keeping it is realistic.

**Can I keep my house in a divorce if I'm not on the mortgage?**
Possibly. If the divorce decree awards you the house, the lender can't call the existing loan due because of that transfer, so you'd put the loan in your name by refinancing. To do that, you would qualify for a new one on your own, and Fannie Mae's rules allow a spouse legally awarded the house in a divorce to refinance it in their own name even if they weren't listed as an owner, when the award is documented.

**How does an equity buyout work?**
The spouse keeping the house refinances into their name only and pays the other spouse their share of the equity. As an illustration, not a quote: a home worth $600,000 with a $300,000 mortgage has $300,000 in equity, each spouse's share is $150,000, and the keeping spouse refinances for $450,000.

**Should I refinance before or after the divorce is final?**
The mortgage analysis comes before; the buyout refinance itself happens once the divorce is final and the decree spells out the terms. Under Fannie Mae's rules, the house also needs to have been jointly owned for at least 12 months before the new loan funds.

**Should I talk to a CDLP before or after my divorce is final?**
Before, ideally while the settlement is being negotiated. The mortgage analysis can start before the divorce is final, and a settlement with mortgage terms that can't be funded is far easier to change before anyone signs it.

**Will divorce hurt my credit score?**
Divorce itself doesn't affect your credit, but the financial disruption around it often does. Late payments on joint accounts, a higher share of income going to debt, and closing joint accounts can all move your scores, so we look at both spouses' credit early.

**Can I use alimony or child support as income to qualify?**
Yes, if it meets the rules. Under Fannie Mae's guideline the payments must be expected to continue at least three years from your application, you need at least six months of full, regular, on-time payments, and a court decree or separation agreement has to show the amount and terms. A one-time payment that evens out the property split (a lump-sum equalization payment) does not count as steady income.

**What if my spouse won't cooperate with the refinance?**
The divorce decree can require cooperation, but timing and logistics still matter. Your attorney writes the decree, and we work with them so it includes specific mortgage language that can be carried out.

**How long after a divorce can I buy a new home?**
You can start as soon as the divorce is final, and the mortgage analysis can begin before that. If the terms are clear enough, the pre-approval can sometimes start during the divorce; the loan itself relies on a final decree that shows your income, debts and obligations.

**Can a lender turn me down because I'm divorced or separated?**
A lender looks at you as an individual applicant, and federal rules protect your right to credit in your own name whatever your marital status. What decides the loan is your own income, credit and debts.
